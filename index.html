              Who it is for, and why telecom first

              • Primary customer: a mobile operator. The operator sits on the channel (SMS and calls), is the brand being impersonated, and is now expected by the regulator to detect, block and report SMS fraud. Banks are a natural second phase: the operator can share confirmed scam links and numbers with them, but a bank product would need a different data model and regulator.
              • Four users. Customers (app, web, forward-to-check), older customers (Arabic, larger text, simple wording), contact-centre agents (check what a customer forwards in seconds) and fraud analysts (campaigns, review queue, takedowns).
              • What the operator gets. Fewer customers defrauded, fewer “is this real?” calls, a brand that is harder to impersonate, and structured reports on new scams that feed its SMS filtering and its reporting to the regulator.

              Architecture

              Scalability and resilience

              ScaleThe check service is stateless, so it scales out behind a load balancer. The rules and classifier are lightweight text processing (the Operator view measures it on your own machine). Reports go through a queue so a spike never slows a customer’s answer. The LLM tier is optional and asynchronous, with a per-month budget cap. These are design targets to prove in a load test before launch.
              Graceful fallbackIf the LLM tier is down, use rules plus classifier. If the classifier is down, use rules alone. If everything is down, say “We can’t check this right now. Use the official app,” and never “looks safe”.
              Data and recoveryIdempotent report ingestion (a retry never double-counts), backups, two availability zones in country, and recovery targets agreed with the operator’s IT team.
              SecurityAll input is treated as hostile: size caps, no clickable links, escaped output, and a hard separation so text pasted by a customer can never instruct the LLM tier. Console access is role-based with an audit log, and takedowns need a second approver.
              OperationsMetrics on latency, flag rate and false alarms; drift alerts when new scam wording appears; every model or rule change runs in shadow mode first.

              Regulation and compliance (Bahrain)

              Personal Data Protection Law (Law No. 30 of 2018)In force since 1 August 2019. It restricts moving personal data out of Bahrain unless the destination is approved, and requires notifying the authority of a breach within 72 hours. Design response: store masked text only (numbers, codes and names blanked), keep reports for a short, fixed period, host in country, ask for consent when a customer submits a report, run a privacy impact assessment before launch, and keep a 72-hour breach runbook. Any LLM provider must be in country or self-hosted.
              TRA “Guidelines for Reducing Fraudulent SMS” (October 2025)Published by the Telecommunications Regulatory Authority with the mobile operators. They expect operators to detect, block and mitigate fraudulent SMS, to educate customers, to provide reporting mechanisms, and to work with a “privacy first” approach under the data protection law. Design response: Sidq maps onto all four: detection, a customer-facing explainer, a reporting button, and a privacy-by-design data model. The report data also supports the operator’s own filtering.
              SIM registration regulation (2015)Context only: the TRA introduced SIM-card registration to reduce fraud and identity theft. Number data can help investigations, but only through the proper channels.
              Banks and other regulatorsBanks are regulated separately. Sharing confirmed scam indicators with a bank would need a data-sharing agreement and legal review, so it is a phase-two item.
              Not legal adviceThe operator’s legal and compliance teams must confirm all of the above before launch.

              Sources: DLA Piper “Data Protection in Bahrain”; Trowers & Hamlins on the 2022 PDPL resolutions; Daily Tribune and GDN coverage of the TRA SMS fraud guidelines (October 2025); TRA SIM-card registration regulation press release (2016).

              Questions the panel is likely to ask

              Where does the customer’s data go?
              Nowhere in this prototype: the page blocks all network access. In production, masked text stays in country with a short retention period and a consent step.
              How do you know it works?
              On synthetic data it catches most scams and shows its misses, and that is the point of the labelled mock set. Real accuracy needs a held-out set of real reports in the shadow phase, before any customer sees a verdict.
              What happens when the model is wrong?
              Customers see reasons, not just a score, so they can judge. Nothing is blocked without a second person approving it, and false alarms on genuine notices are tracked as a headline metric.
              Why would an operator pay for this?
              Fraud losses, support calls, and trust in the brand. The cost model above shows the break-even in incidents prevented per month, to be filled with the operator’s own numbers.
              Does it fit our existing fraud stack?
              It adds to it: confirmed links and senders feed the operator’s SMS filtering and sender-ID registry, and the customer reports give the fraud team early warning of new campaigns.
              Why not just use a large language model?
              Cost, speed, privacy and explainability. Rules and a small classifier answer in milliseconds and can run in country. An LLM tier is an optional add-on for call transcripts and unusual wording.
              Is it only for telecom?
              Telecom first, because the operator owns the channel and is the brand being copied. Banks are a natural second phase through shared scam indicators.

              The problem

              People get calls, messages and social media accounts that pretend to be their telecom operator’s support. The goal is always the same: rush you into sharing a code, installing an app or paying a fee. In the moment it is hard to tell the difference, and older customers are hit hardest.

              What Sidq does

              1. ReadMessage, transcript, and optionally the sender.
              2. Spot warning signsUrgency, requests for codes or card details, bait, odd links, remote-access apps, secrecy.
              3. Check the wordingA small trained classifier compares it with scam and genuine examples.
              4. ExplainA rating, the exact phrases behind it, and why each matters.
              5. ActPlain next steps and a ready report for the operator.
              • Reassuring wording never wins. A message that asks for a code or remote access is never rated as clean, even if it copies “we will never ask for your OTP”.
              • Look-alike detection. Fake accounts and links such as @operatorx_suport, operatorx-billing.top or a Cyrillic “о” hidden in a Latin word are caught.
              • Hidden-character tricks. Invisible and direction-changing characters used to dodge filters are removed and flagged.
              • Caller ID can be faked. A sender that matches the official list is noted, but never treated as proof.
              • Private by design. The page has no network access at all (it blocks itself with a content security policy), and nothing is stored.

              Where AI is used

              • A Naive Bayes text classifier, trained in your browser on a small set of scam and genuine messages in English and Arabic. Those examples are synthetic, written for this demo.
              • Explainable rules cover the warning signs, so a customer sees why something was flagged.
              • Next step in production: train on real customer and operator reports, add a language model to read call transcripts, and show a verified-support badge in the operator’s app.

              Why it matters to an operator

              • Operators are the brand being impersonated, so protecting customers protects trust in the network.
              • The report button gives the fraud team structured data on new scam wording and fake accounts.
              • Plain language, Arabic support and larger text make it usable by older customers.

              Limits

              • The trained model is small and synthetic, so it can miss new wording. That is why the warning-sign rules carry most of the weight.
              • It checks text. It does not listen to live calls.
              • The official contact list here is a demo.

              New in this version

              • Seal. The operator stamps genuine messages with a short code computed over the exact text. A fake cannot carry a valid one, and an edited message fails. The demo uses a shared secret that is visible on this page, so it is not secure here; production uses public-key signatures, message IDs and expiry.
              • Live call. The transcript is re-scored after every line, so the warning comes during the call. It takes typed or pasted lines. Speech-to-text is not built.
              • Case file. A report becomes one case with a reference and a handoff summary, so the customer does not repeat themselves across channels. It lives in this page only.
              • Accessibility. Read aloud (the device’s own voices, which may not include Arabic), simple view, high contrast, larger text, a four-question mode for people who cannot paste, and visual alerts for people with hearing loss. Not yet tested with real users or screen-reader users.
              • Check the agent. The customer checks the person on the phone, not only the message: the agent’s console shows a six-digit code, tied to the case, that changes every 30 seconds and is read out to the customer. A scammer who does not hold the operator’s secret cannot produce it. The demo secret is visible in this page, so in production the check lives on the operator’s side.
              • Link inspector. Every link is compared with the official address and shown as a character-by-character diff, so “operatorx-billing.top” visibly is not “operatorx.example”.
              • Case status. Received, under review, number or link flagged, closed. The next agent sees the status on any channel.
              • Practise. Six made-up messages to decide on, then the checker’s answer and reasons. Aimed at families and schools.
              • Screenshots. A screenshot can be added by clicking, dropping or pasting. It is kept with the report. The demo does not read the words in it; that needs a text-recognition service on the operator’s side.
              • Speak it. An optional button turns speech into text using the browser’s own speech service (in Chrome the audio goes to Google). Sidq does not send or keep it. The checker itself sends nothing anywhere.
              • Learn. A 30-second tip you can listen to, and ten made-up messages to judge.
              • Red team. The checker rewrites scams with evasion tricks and reports how many it still catches, before and after hardening. The fixes were written against the same tricks, so the “after” figures are optimistic.
